1. Executive Summary  
The three-day workshop covered a broad set of themes around deepfake detection, digital forensics, cybersecurity, 
AI-based media analysis, and emerging research directions. The sessions combined real-world case studies with 
technical concepts, investigative methods, and research-oriented discussions.  
The notes indicate a progression from understanding deepfake incidents and their societal and security implications, 
to practical investigation and forensic analysis, and finally to advanced machine-learning research topics such as 
domain adaptation, generalization, multimodal analysis, uncertainty, and out of distribution detection.

6. Cross-Day Analysis and Key Learnings  
6.1 Progression of the Workshop  
1. Day 1 – Understand the threat: deepfakes, attacks, real-world incidents, visual indicators, and investigation.  
2. Day 2 – Investigate the incident: practical evidence collection, online traces, IP/domain analysis, tools, and 
forensic workflows.  
3. Day 3 – Build better systems: advanced ML research, domain generalization, multimodal analysis, explainability, 
authentication, and OOD detection.  
6.2 Major Themes Across Speakers  
• 
Deepfakes are a security and forensic problem, not only an image-generation problem.  
• 
Source and provenance verification are critical complements to pixel-level or model-based detection.  
• 
Real-world case reconstruction helps bridge the gap between technical models and actual investigations.  
• 
Forensic AI must be robust to dataset shifts, new manipulation methods, unfamiliar sources, and openset 
conditions.  
• 
Multimodal evidence—image, video, audio, metadata, source information, and network traces—can provide 
stronger investigative conclusions.  
• 
Explainability matters because forensic conclusions need to be understandable and defensible.  
• 
Locally relevant datasets and realistic benchmarks are important for reliable deployment.  
• 
Cryptographic and authentication mechanisms can strengthen trust in digital evidence.  
6.3 Practical Takeaways  
• 
Do not rely on a single visual artifact to declare content fake.  
• 
Check the source, provenance, context, and surrounding evidence.  
• 
Use multiple forensic indicators such as lighting, boundaries, gaze/blinking, frequency patterns, and other 
inconsistencies.  
• 
Treat suspicious links, domains, IP addresses, and online traces as potential evidence in a broader cyber 
investigation.  
• 
For AI systems, evaluate cross-dataset performance rather than only in-dataset accuracy.  
• 
Include open-set/OOD evaluation so systems can handle unfamiliar manipulations.  
• 
Maintain an evidence-oriented workflow so technical findings can support a complete incident reconstruction.

7. Conclusion  
Across the three days, the workshop presented a complete perspective on deepfake and digital-forensic challenges—
from understanding how attacks occur, to investigating real incidents, examining evidence, and designing more 
robust AI systems. The speaker sessions collectively demonstrated that reliable deepfake forensics requires a 
combination of investigation, source verification, computer vision, multimodal learning, cybersecurity, cryptography, 
and careful evaluation of model robustness.  
The overall learning can be summarized as: detect the manipulation, verify the source, reconstruct the incident, 
validate the evidence, and design AI systems that remain reliable when the data, domain, or attack method changes.
